LinkedIn AI-generated comments are about to lose much of their visibility. LinkedIn Chief Product Officer Hari Srinivasan announced in a [LinkedIn post](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/hsrinivasan1_we-are-not-slowing-down-on-reducing-ai-slop-share-7527062264584245249-710j/) that the platform has updated its detection and classifiers for comments, so suspected AI comments get less exposure in the feed. [Social Media Today reported the change on November 15, 2026](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/linkedin-cracks-down-on-ai-generated-comments/874707/).

This affects anyone who uses tools to post quick, generic replies at scale: growth hackers, some agencies, and even executives whose teams "engage" on their behalf. It also matters to creators, because LinkedIn says notifications will focus on comments people are likely to care about.

For brands that rely on LinkedIn for B2B reach, comments have long been a way to get seen on other people's posts. That shortcut now works far less well if the comment reads like a machine wrote it.

## What LinkedIn changed for AI-generated comments

According to Social Media Today's report, the update has three parts:

| Area | Before | Now |
|---|---|---|
| Comment ranking | Generic AI replies could sit high in the thread | New classifiers judge relevance and flag suspected AI comments as less valuable |
| Default view | "Most relevant" showed a mix | "Most relevant" should show fewer AI comments |
| Creator notifications | Alerts for many comments | Alerts only for "high-quality comments you're likely to care about" |

Social Media Today also reports that LinkedIn is re-ranking suspected AI comments rather than removing them. They stay on the post, but people are much less likely to see them unless they change the sort order.

## The test result and rollout

LinkedIn reported "a >20% reduction in views of slop comments" in testing, per Social Media Today, with the change set to reach everyone soon. A [TechJuice report from November 16, 2026](https://www.techjuice.pk/linkedin-downgrades-ai-generated-comments/) describes the same figures.

LinkedIn has not published a full list of signals its classifiers use, so nobody outside LinkedIn knows exactly what counts as "AI slop". Treat any tool or guru claiming to beat the classifier with caution.

This builds on earlier work. Social Media Today notes that in August 2026, LinkedIn added an option to report AI slop on posts in the feed, and more than one million people used it within the first three weeks. LinkedIn has also renamed its AI post enhancement feature as a "proofreader", which signals the line it wants to draw between AI assisted writing and AI generated filler.

## Why LinkedIn cares about comment quality

LinkedIn has been reworking how its feed matches content to people, as we explained in our piece on [LinkedIn profile relevance and the feed](/linkedin-profile-relevance-feed-llm/). Comments are part of that system. If threads fill up with identical "Great insights, thanks for sharing!" replies, real discussion gets buried and people stop reading.

## How brands should comment on LinkedIn now

![Checklist for commenting on LinkedIn after the AI comment change](/images/infographics/ig-linkedin-ai-generated-comments-demoted-2.webp)

These are Brandella's recommendations, not LinkedIn guidance.

1. **Switch off auto comment tools.** Any extension or service that posts comments for you is now a risk to your visibility and reputation.
2. **Comment less, but better.** Five thoughtful comments a day beat fifty generic ones. Add a fact, an example or a question.
3. **Be specific to the post.** Mention a detail from what the author wrote. Generic praise is the easiest thing for a classifier to spot.
4. **Use AI as an editor, not a writer.** Draft your own point, then let a tool fix grammar if you want. That matches the "proofreader" framing LinkedIn now uses.
5. **Brief executives and employees.** If a team manages comments for leaders, agree simple rules: real views, plain language, no copy and paste.
6. **Track comment driven results.** Watch profile visits and replies that follow your comments. If they drop, review what you are posting.
7. **Clean up your own threads.** Reply to real questions on your posts and ignore spammy replies so the useful discussion rises.

## FAQ about LinkedIn AI-generated comments

### Does LinkedIn delete AI-generated comments?

No. Social Media Today reports that LinkedIn re-ranks suspected AI comments lower instead of removing them.

### How much did views of AI comments drop in testing?

LinkedIn reported a more than 20% reduction in views of slop comments in testing, according to Social Media Today.

### Can I still use AI to help write LinkedIn comments?

LinkedIn has not banned AI help. Its classifiers target low value, suspected AI comments, so using AI only to polish your own original point is the safer approach.

### When will the change reach all users?

Social Media Today reports that LinkedIn said the change would roll out to everyone soon, without a specific date.
