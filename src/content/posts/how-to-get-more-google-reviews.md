Review quotas and staff-name solicitation are now explicit violations, and the enforcement caught up the same week the policy changed.</p>

<p>A lot of businesses aren't trying to game the system. They inherited a review request script from an agency, a kiosk from a vendor, or a "ask happy customers first" habit that felt harmless. Some of that playbook quietly became a policy violation without anyone noticing.</p>

<p>Google's principle hasn't shifted: every review has to reflect a real, unbiased experience. What changed is how specifically Google now defines manipulation, and how aggressively its systems detect it, a shift <a href="https://www.mckinneycv.com/resources/google-review-policy-guide/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">McKinney Creative Ventures' plain-English guide to the policy</a> documents in detail. <h2 id="core-rule"><span class="bx-num">01</span>The Core Rule Behind Every Google Review Policy</h2>
<div class="bx-rule"></div>
<p>Everything Google enforces comes down to one requirement buried in its Maps user-generated content rules: a review has to be based on a real experience and accurately represent the business. Google's own <a href="https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114?hl=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Maps user-generated content policy</a> prohibits content that isn't grounded in a genuine interaction.</p>

<p>If a review is invented, incentivized, written by someone with a stake in the business, or coached into existence, it fails that test regardless of how glowing it sounds. That single principle is what every specific rule below traces back to.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.kukui.com/google-changed-the-rules-on-reviews" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Kukui's June 2026 breakdown</a> of the policy shift is worth internalizing: several of these practices were always against the rules, they're simply being actively enforced now in a way they weren't before.</p>

<h2 id="april-2026-update"><span class="bx-num">02</span>What Changed in Google's April 2026 Review Policy Update</h2>
<div class="bx-rule"></div>
<p>On April 16, 2026, Google published a Maps blog post outlining new protections for businesses, and the following day, two new clauses landed in the rating manipulation section of its Prohibited and Restricted Content policy. <a href="https://www.replyonthefly.com/blog/google-review-policy-update-2026" target="_blank" rel="noopener">ReplyOnTheFly's detailed writeup</a> traces the timeline closely, and the two additions are specific.</p>

<p>The first bans merchants from requesting that staff solicit a certain number of reviews, closing the door on the "bring in ten reviews this week" quota that used to be common advice. The second bans requesting that staff solicit reviews naming a specific employee.</p>

<div class="bx-note">
<span class="lbl">What This Doesn't Ban</span>
<p><a href="https://www.threechaptermedia.com/blog/google-review-policy-2026" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Threechaptermedia's coverage</a> of the update makes an important clarification: Google didn't ban customers from mentioning staff by name organically. A customer writing "Jake was fantastic" on their own is completely fine. What's prohibited is the business engineering that mention through coached requests or campaigns.</p>

<h2 id="review-gating"><span class="bx-num">03</span>Review Gating Is Banned and Now Actively Enforced</h2>
<div class="bx-rule"></div>
<p>Review gating means pre-screening customers by sentiment before sending them to leave a public review, usually through a satisfaction survey that routes four- and five-star responses to Google while sending anything lower to a private form.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.mainstreethost.com/blog/google-review-policy-update-april-2026/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Mainstreethost's coverage</a> of the update frames the goal clearly: reviews need to happen naturally, not through a filtered or gamified process. Every customer has to get the same request, regardless of how you expect them to respond.</p>

<h2 id="incentivized-reviews"><span class="bx-num">04</span>Incentivized Reviews Are Never Allowed, Positive or Negative</h2>
<div class="bx-rule"></div>
<p>Offering anything of value in exchange for a review, a discount, a free service, loyalty points, or a giveaway entry, violates Google's policy outright. The incentive taints the review whether the outcome ends up positive or negative.</p>

<p>That ban extends further than people expect. <a href="https://www.socialpilot.co/reviews/blogs/how-to-get-google-reviews" target="_blank" rel="noopener">SocialPilot's 2026 review tactics guide</a> points out that offering an incentive to revise or remove a negative review is prohibited too, not just incentives tied to leaving a new one.</p>

<h2 id="on-premises"><span class="bx-num">05</span>On-Premises Pressure and Review Kiosks Are Explicitly Banned</h2>
<div class="bx-rule"></div>
<p>Google's policy states merchants shouldn't require or pressure customers to leave a review while still on the business's premises. That single line is the basis for the crackdown on review kiosks and shared tablets sitting at checkout counters.</p>

<p>Kukui's breakdown explains why these devices draw extra scrutiny: reviews clustered from one shared device and IP address look like manipulation to Google's spam systems, even when every individual review is genuine. The compliant pattern flips the timing entirely, customers review later, on their own device, after they've left.</p>

<h2 id="insider-reviews"><span class="bx-num">06</span>Insider Reviews and the FTC's Fake Review Rule</h2>
<div class="bx-rule"></div>
<p>Reviews from anyone connected to the business, owners, current or former employees, contractors, family, or close friends, fall under Google's conflict-of-interest prohibition and can't be published.</p>

<p>In the United States, this stopped being purely a platform issue back in October 2024. The <a href="https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials" target="_blank" rel="noopener">FTC's fake reviews rule</a> makes it unlawful to buy or sell fake reviews and covers undisclosed insider reviews, with civil penalties available against businesses that knowingly violate it. A staff or family review campaign now carries legal exposure on top of the platform risk.</p>

<h2 id="faq"><span class="bx-num">12</span>FAQs on How to Get More Google Reviews Compliantly</h2>
<div class="bx-rule"></div>

<div class="bx-faq" id="bxFaq">
  <button class="bx-q">What changed in Google's April 2026 review policy update?<span class="ic">+</span></button>
  <div class="bx-a"><p>Google added two explicit bans: staff review quotas and merchants soliciting reviews that name a specific employee. Both were rolled into the rating manipulation section on April 17, 2026.</p></div>
  <button class="bx-q">Can a customer still mention an employee's name in a review?<span class="ic">+</span></button>
  <div class="bx-a"><p>Yes. An organic, unprompted mention is fine. What's banned is the business coaching customers to name staff or running a campaign built around it.</p></div>
  <button class="bx-q">Is offering a discount for a Google review ever allowed?<span class="ic">+</span></button>
  <div class="bx-a"><p>No. Any incentive, discount, gift, or giveaway entry tied to a review is prohibited, whether the resulting review is positive or negative.</p></div>
  <button class="bx-q">Are in-store review kiosks against Google's policy?<span class="ic">+</span></button>
  <div class="bx-a"><p>Yes. Requiring or pressuring customers to review while on the premises is explicitly banned, and shared devices also trigger spam-detection flags.</p></div>
  <button class="bx-q">Can employees or family members review my business?<span class="ic">+</span></button>
  <div class="bx-a"><p>No. These fall under Google's conflict-of-interest rule, and in the US, undisclosed insider reviews also carry exposure under the FTC's 2024 fake reviews rule.</p></div>
