<p>Quick one before we get into it. You have probably stared at a keyword tool screen with two numbers side by side and felt stuck on which one should actually decide what you write next.</p>

<p class="bx-lede"><em>Picture this: you type a topic into your keyword tool and get back a volume column and a difficulty column, and they point in opposite directions. The high volume term looks scary to rank for, the easy term barely gets searched. Understanding search volume vs keyword difficulty, and how each number is actually produced behind the scenes, is what turns that confusing screen into a real shortlist.</em></p>

<div class="bx-section-head">
  <span class="bx-num">01</span>
  <h2 class="bx-h2" id="search-volume-vs-keyword-difficulty-what-each-number-actually-measures">Search Volume vs Keyword Difficulty: What Each Number Actually Measures</h2>
  <div class="bx-rule"></div>
</div>

<p>Search volume is an estimate of monthly demand, how many times people type a given phrase into a search engine. Keyword difficulty is an estimate of competition, how hard it looks to break into the top results for that phrase. They answer two completely different questions.</p>

<p>A keyword can have huge demand and be nearly impossible to rank for, or tiny demand and be wide open. Neither number tells you the full story on its own, and that is exactly why so many people get keyword prioritization wrong. Weighing search volume vs keyword difficulty together, rather than picking one and ignoring the other, is the actual skill worth learning here.</p>

<p>The tricky part is that both numbers are estimates built by different companies using different data and different math, which is where most of the confusion starts.</p>

<div class="bx-section-head">
  <span class="bx-num">02</span>
  <h2 class="bx-h2" id="how-search-volume-is-actually-estimated">How Search Volume Is Actually Estimated</h2>
  <div class="bx-rule"></div>
</div>

<p>Most keyword tools, including the big third party platforms, ultimately trace their search volume figures back to Google Ads data, the same backend that powers Google Keyword Planner. That makes Keyword Planner the closest thing the industry has to a real data source, even for people who never run an ad.</p>

<p>Google itself is upfront that these are estimates, not a live traffic counter. Its own help documentation for Keyword Planner states plainly that search volume statistics are rounded and that actual search numbers are constantly fluctuating due to <a href="https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/3022575?hl=en">seasonality, current events, and other factors</a>, so a monthly figure is a snapshot, not a guarantee.</p>

<p>Third party tools then layer their own clickstream and panel data on top of that Google Ads baseline to smooth out gaps, which is part of why two tools rarely show the exact same number for the same term even when both are reasonably accurate.</p>

<div class="bx-section-head">
  <span class="bx-num">03</span>
  <h2 class="bx-h2" id="why-you-usually-see-a-range-not-an-exact-number">Why You Usually See a Range, Not an Exact Number</h2>
  <div class="bx-rule"></div>
</div>

<p>Open Keyword Planner without an active ad campaign and you get wide buckets like 0 to 10, 10 to 100, 100 to 1K, all the way up to 1M plus. That is genuinely useful for spotting a trend, but useless for comparing two mid sized keywords against each other.</p>

<p>Run an active campaign and Google unlocks roughly 60 narrower buckets instead of 7, according to independent analysis of real <a href="https://www.authoritas.com/blog/understanding-googles-search-volume-buckets-a-deep-dive-into-how-search-volumes-really-work">Google search volume bucket data</a>. The same analysis found bucket width scales with size, typically needing an 18 to 23 percent real change before a keyword jumps to the next bucket, so a term sitting at 1.83 million searches needs roughly a 50 percent swing before Keyword Planner shows any movement at all.</p>

<p>Several SEO writers have flagged the same practical headache from the other side. One popular Keyword Planner walkthrough bluntly describes the monthly figure as a range, calling it <a href="https://backlinko.com/google-keyword-planner">not a super accurate indicator of search volume</a> on its own, which is exactly why serious keyword prioritization never leans on one tool alone.</p>

<div class="bx-section-head">
  <span class="bx-num">04</span>
  <h2 class="bx-h2" id="how-keyword-difficulty-scores-are-actually-calculated">How Keyword Difficulty Scores Are Actually Calculated</h2>
  <div class="bx-rule"></div>
</div>

<p>Keyword difficulty tries to answer a harder question than volume does, and tools disagree on the math far more because of it. Ahrefs keeps its approach simple on purpose. It looks at the top 10 ranking pages for a keyword and counts the referring domains pointing at each one, then plots that on a 0 to 100 scale where the steps get steeper as you climb, per <a href="https://ahrefs.com/keyword-difficulty">Ahrefs own explanation of its Keyword Difficulty metric</a>. Reaching a score near 40 usually takes somewhere around 56 referring domains, while a score near 90 can require roughly 756.</p>

<p>Semrush takes a heavier approach. Its updated Keyword Difficulty formula pulls the median referring domains and median Authority Score from the top 10 results, then blends in search volume, SERP features like featured snippets, whether the term is branded, and page word count, each carrying its own weight, as <a href="https://www.semrush.com/blog/most-accurate-keyword-difficulty/">Semrush documents in its own methodology breakdown</a>. Referring domains still dominate the formula, but the extra inputs mean Semrush and Ahrefs are rarely starting from the same ingredients.</p>

<div class="bx-section-head">
  <span class="bx-num">05</span>
  <h2 class="bx-h2" id="why-the-same-keyword-shows-different-scores-on-different-tools">Why the Same Keyword Shows Different Scores on Different Tools</h2>
  <div class="bx-rule"></div>
</div>

<p>Ahrefs puts it plainly on its own blog. These tools all use the same 0 to 100 scale, but each one calculates it differently, which is really the whole story in a single sentence from <a href="https://ahrefs.com/blog/keyword-difficulty/">Ahrefs own keyword difficulty guide</a>. A score of 35 in one tool and 52 in another for the identical phrase does not mean either number is wrong, it means they measured different things with different link indexes.</p>

<p>Part of the gap comes from the metrics feeding the formulas in the first place. Domain Authority, Authority Score, and similar labels are not figures Google publishes. They are proprietary estimates each SEO software company builds from its own crawl of the web, and one widely read keyword guide points out that this is exactly why scores <a href="https://www.wordstream.com/blog/ws/2018/09/12/keyword-difficulty">vary from product to product</a> even when they are all trying to describe the same ranking page.</p>

<p>The practical takeaway is simple. Never compare a raw difficulty number across two different tools. Track it consistently inside one platform instead, and treat the number as a rough guide rather than a guarantee.</p>

<div class="bx-fact">
  <span class="bx-fact-label">Did You Know</span>
  <p>On the Ahrefs scale, a keyword difficulty score of roughly 40 corresponds to about 56 referring domains needed to break the top 10, while a score near 90 can require around 756, a jump that shows just how uneven the 0 to 100 scale really is. <a href="https://ahrefs.com/keyword-difficulty">Ahrefs</a> publishes the underlying referring domain estimates behind every score band.</p>
</div>

<div class="bx-section-head">
  <span class="bx-num">06</span>
  <h2 class="bx-h2" id="a-practical-framework-search-volume-vs-keyword-difficulty-plus-intent">A Practical Framework: Search Volume vs Keyword Difficulty, Plus Intent</h2>
  <div class="bx-rule"></div>
</div>

<p>The strongest keyword research advice is not to chase the biggest volume column you can find. It is to look for the overlap of three signals at once, decent demand, a difficulty score you can realistically beat, and a phrase that matches what the searcher actually wants next.</p>

<p>Search Engine Land makes this concrete with a donor advised fund example. A bank chasing the broad, high volume phrase pulls in curious browsers, while a lower volume, intent rich phrase like opening an account pulls in people ready to act, and the <a href="https://www.searchenginejournal.com/ask-an-seo-optimize-keywords-with-high-search-volume-or-competition/543214/">intent match ends up mattering more than the raw search count</a> in almost every case worth studying.</p>

<p>One SEO columnist goes further and argues that competition scores themselves are simply numbers invented by software vendors, not something search engines use when ranking a page, and recommends building topic clusters around real user questions instead of chasing a single metric, a view worth sitting with the next time a <a href="https://searchengineland.com/rethinking-keyword-strategy-optimize-search-intent-442864">tool's intent and relevance signals disagree with its volume column</a>.</p>

<p>Put plainly, when you weigh search volume vs keyword difficulty side by side with real intent, you end up with a shortlist worth actually writing for, not just a spreadsheet full of impressive looking numbers.</p>

<div class="bx-tip">
  <p class="bx-tip-label">Tip</p>
  <p>Build a simple score, volume divided by difficulty, then manually downgrade anything whose top results clearly do not match your intent. If you are researching dozens of terms at once, our <a href="/python-keyword-research-automation/">keyword research automation guide</a> shows how to run that scoring at scale instead of row by row in a spreadsheet.</p>
</div>

<div class="bx-section-head">
  <span class="bx-num">07</span>
  <h2 class="bx-h2" id="common-mistakes-when-prioritizing-keywords">Common Mistakes When Prioritizing Keywords</h2>
  <div class="bx-rule"></div>
</div>

<p>Most prioritization mistakes come from treating one of these two numbers as the whole answer. A few show up again and again across new sites.</p>

<p>Newer sites especially tend to aim for head terms that look impressive in a spreadsheet, then stall for months because the difficulty score was never realistic for their current authority. Pairing a keyword tool with a general toolkit, like the options in our <a href="/10-best-free-seo-tools-every-marketer-should-use/">free SEO tools roundup</a>, helps catch that mismatch earlier.</p>

<div class="bx-checklist">
<li><b>Chasing the biggest volume number on the page.</b> Filter by a difficulty score your site can realistically beat before you even look at volume.</li>
<li><b>Comparing raw difficulty scores between two different tools.</b> Pick one tool and judge every keyword against its own scale only.</li>
<li><b>Ignoring what searchers actually want.</b> Open the top results yourself and check the format and intent before writing a word.</li>
<li><b>Skipping low volume, highly specific phrases.</b> A cluster of smaller terms often adds up to more traffic than one big head term ever delivers.</li>
<li><b>Never auditing which pages already rank for anything.</b> Crawl your own site with a tool like <a href="/screaming-frog-seo-spider-beginners-guide/">Screaming Frog</a> to see what you can simply improve rather than write from zero.</li>
</div>

<div class="bx-faq">
  <div class="bx-faq-item open">
    <button class="bx-faq-q"><span class="bx-faq-num">1</span>What is search volume vs keyword difficulty, and which one should guide my choices?</button>
    <div class="bx-faq-a"><p>Search volume estimates monthly demand for a phrase, while keyword difficulty estimates how hard it is to rank for it. Neither one should guide your choice alone. Weighing search volume vs keyword difficulty together, alongside real search intent, is what actually produces a workable content list.</p></div>
  </div>
  <div class="bx-faq-item open">
    <button class="bx-faq-q"><span class="bx-faq-num">2</span>Where does Google Keyword Planner actually get its search volume numbers from?</button>
    <div class="bx-faq-a"><p>The figures come from aggregated Google Ads query data. Google rounds the numbers and notes they shift with seasonality and current events, which is why the same keyword can read differently depending on the month you check it.</p></div>
  </div>
  <div class="bx-faq-item open">
    <button class="bx-faq-q"><span class="bx-faq-num">3</span>Why does Keyword Planner only show a range instead of an exact number?</button>
    <div class="bx-faq-a"><p>Without an active ad campaign, Google groups results into roughly seven wide buckets. Running a live campaign unlocks closer to 60 narrower buckets, which is why agencies and advertisers often see tighter ranges than a casual visitor browsing the free tool.</p></div>
  </div>
  <div class="bx-faq-item open">
    <button class="bx-faq-q"><span class="bx-faq-num">4</span>Why do Ahrefs and Semrush show different difficulty scores for the same keyword?</button>
    <div class="bx-faq-a"><p>Ahrefs scores difficulty almost entirely on referring domains to the top 10 pages. Semrush blends referring domains with Authority Score, search volume, SERP features, and more. Different ingredients produce different numbers even on the identical 0 to 100 scale.</p></div>
  </div>
  <div class="bx-faq-item open">
    <button class="bx-faq-q"><span class="bx-faq-num">5</span>What should a new or low authority site target first?</button>
    <div class="bx-faq-a"><p>Favor lower difficulty, specific phrases with clear buyer or informational intent over broad head terms. Smaller wins build the authority and internal links that eventually make the harder, higher volume phrases reachable.</p></div>
  </div>
</div>
